How founders can plan a month of LinkedIn posts from their own expertise

By Pluto Theory · 25 November 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: to plan a month of LinkedIn posts, pick three to five topics (pillars) that come from the questions your customers keep asking you, aim for two to three posts a week, give each week one post that teaches, one that tells a story and, if you can, one that takes a position, and keep a running list of raw material from your real week. Draft one week at a time, review what worked once a month, and keep the rhythm small enough to hold for six months.

This guide is for founders and owner-managers who know a LinkedIn presence would help but start every week from a blank page. It covers choosing pillars, setting a realistic rhythm, finding material in your own work, a template you can copy, and how to review what you posted. It also covers where automation helps, and where LinkedIn's rules and common sense say it should stop.

Start with the reader, not the algorithm

A content plan fails most often because it starts from "what should I post" instead of "who should read this and what should they do afterwards". Write two sentences before anything else:

  • Who: the type of person whose attention is worth having. For example, a clinic owner choosing a software vendor, or a head of marketing at a D2C brand.
  • Next step: what you would like them to do after reading a few of your posts. Reply, visit your site, book a call, or simply remember your name when the need comes up.

Everything else in the plan is a choice about those two sentences. A post that does not serve either one is a candidate to drop, however well it might perform.

Choose three to five content pillars

A pillar is a topic you will return to again and again. Several guides we read in November 2026 (including ones from ContentIn, Later and a startup CEO template) suggest three as a sensible minimum and five as a practical maximum. Fewer than three gets repetitive; more than five makes it hard for a reader to say what you stand for.

Good pillars for a founder usually come from this list. Pick the ones where you have something to say:

PillarWhat it coversA hypothetical example
Customer problemsThe questions and mistakes you see again and againA physiotherapy clinic owner explains the three questions new patients ask before booking
How we do itYour method, checklist or process, in plain termsA founder walks through how an enquiry is handled in the first hour
OpinionA position on how your industry should workA view on why discounting too early weakens a brand
Building the businessDecisions, trade-offs and lessons from running itWhy you stopped offering one service, and what you learned
People and proofYour team, customers and results you are free to shareA short note on a lesson from a customer conversation, shared with permission

If you are early and have no results you can share yet, say so and lean on the first four. Readers forgive a young business; they do not forgive made-up numbers.

Set a rhythm you can keep

There is no authoritative study that settles the ideal number of posts. The guides we read in November 2026 are mostly written by software vendors, and their figures conflict. They do agree on the shape: consistent beats frequent. Reported suggestions range from two to three posts a week (the figure attributed to LinkedIn's own marketing account) up to three to five for founders who have the time. One guide makes the point directly: posting three times a week for six months does more than posting daily for two weeks and then going quiet.

Our suggestion for a busy founder is the smaller end:

  1. Start at two posts a week for the first month, on days you can actually protect.
  2. Add a third only when two feel easy for four weeks in a row.
  3. Spend 15 to 20 minutes a day on comments, replying to people who respond to you and adding a useful thought on posts from your customers and peers. Several of the guides we read point to the first hour after posting as the time when replies matter most.

Rotate formats rather than repeating one. Plain text posts, a document (carousel) post, a short native video and an occasional photo from a real event all work for different jobs. Posts that send readers off LinkedIn to a link tend to be described as weaker for reach, so put the useful part in the post itself and add a link only when it helps.

Find the material in your own week

Founders rarely run out of expertise. They run out of captured expertise. The fix is a running list, not a brainstorm on Sunday night. Keep a note on your phone and add to it whenever one of these happens:

  • A customer asks a question you have answered before.
  • You change your mind about something, or a decision goes wrong.
  • You explain your process to someone new and notice which part confused them.
  • You disagree with advice you read.
  • You turn down a piece of work, and know why.

Once a week, turn the best two or three entries into posts. A useful test for each: could a competitor have written this sentence? If so, add the detail only you know, such as the number, the example, the thing that went wrong.

A month on one page

Here is a simple four-week template at two posts a week. Swap in your own pillars:

WeekPost 1Post 2
1Customer problems: the question you hear most, answered in fullBuilding the business: a decision and what it taught you
2How we do it: your checklist or first-hour processOpinion: a position you would defend in front of a customer
3Customer problems: a common mistake and the fixPeople and proof: a lesson from a customer conversation, shared with permission
4How we do it: one thing you changed in your process this monthReview: what you learned from the month, and what comes next

Each post needs three parts: a first line that tells the reader what they will get, a body with one idea and one specific example, and an ending that either asks a real question or says what to do next. Write them in your own words. Readers can tell when a post was written for the algorithm.

Review once a month, in 20 minutes

One of the guides we read suggests waiting at least four weeks of data before changing your frequency. Take that as a rule. At the end of the month, open LinkedIn's analytics for your posts and write down:

  • Which two posts got the most meaningful replies, not just the most reactions.
  • Which pillar they belonged to.
  • Whether any post led to a message, a call or a customer mentioning it.
  • One thing to drop, one thing to repeat.

Then adjust the next month's template. This beats guessing at the algorithm, and it keeps the plan tied to the reader and next step you wrote down in the first section.

Where automation helps, and where it stops

Planning and drafting are repetitive in a useful way, which makes them good candidates for automation: collecting your notes into a topic list, proposing a month of topics against your pillars, drafting a first version in your voice for you to edit, reminding you when a draft is due and keeping a calendar. The review step stays with you, because a post is your public statement.

The line is automation of activity on LinkedIn itself. LinkedIn's rules do not allow bots that browse, send connection requests or message people for you, and accounts that do this risk restriction. Questions people ask

How many times a week should a founder post on LinkedIn?

Most guides we read in November 2026 land between two and four posts a week. LinkedIn's own marketing account is reported to suggest at least two to three a week. A cadence you can keep for six months beats a daily burst that stops after a fortnight, so start at two and add a third only when the first two feel easy.

How many content pillars do I need?

Three to five. Fewer than three gets repetitive, and more than five spreads you too thin for readers to know what you stand for. Pick pillars from the questions your customers ask you most often, not from what sounds impressive.

How far ahead should I plan LinkedIn posts?

Plan a month of topics and draft one week at a time. A month gives you enough variety to see the shape of it, and weekly drafting leaves room for something that happened on Tuesday to become Thursday's post.

Can I automate my LinkedIn posts?

You can automate the planning, drafting and reminders, and use scheduling routes LinkedIn supports. You should not use tools that automate browsing, connection requests or messages, which LinkedIn's rules do not allow. See our guide to LinkedIn automation rules for the detail.
