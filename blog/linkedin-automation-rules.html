LinkedIn automation rules: what LinkedIn allows, and what gets accounts restricted

By Pluto Theory · 24 November 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: LinkedIn does not allow third-party software that automates activity on its site, including bots, scrapers and browser extensions that send connection requests, send messages, visit profiles or collect data. Accounts that use such tools risk being restricted or shut down. What you can automate is the work around LinkedIn: planning topics, drafting posts from your own expertise, reviewing them and keeping a queue, then publishing yourself or through a route LinkedIn supports.

This guide is for founders and lean marketing teams who want to use LinkedIn without risking the profile they have spent years building. It sets out what LinkedIn's own help pages say, which kinds of tools fall on the wrong side, what a restriction looks like, and a safe split between what software does and what a person does. It is not legal advice, and LinkedIn can change its rules, so check the current pages linked from LinkedIn's Help Center before you pick a tool.

What LinkedIn's rules actually say

LinkedIn's help page on prohibited software and extensions says the company does not permit third-party software, including crawlers, bots, browser plug-ins and browser extensions, that scrapes the site, changes how it looks, or automates activity on it. A separate help page on automated activity explains the reason: software that automates tasks can export or scrape data from LinkedIn, sometimes without a member's consent, and it affects how authentic the platform feels to everyone else. The LinkedIn User Agreement also tells members not to use bots or other unauthorised automated methods to access the service or collect data.

Two details are worth noticing. First, the rule is written by category, not by brand. LinkedIn does not publish a list of banned tools, so "this tool is popular" or "other people use it" tells you nothing. Second, the rule covers what the software does on the platform, such as clicking, sending, visiting and collecting. It says nothing against thinking, writing and planning outside LinkedIn.

What counts as automation on LinkedIn

A quick way to sort a tool: does it act inside LinkedIn as if it were you, or does it work somewhere else and hand you the result?

ActivityWhere it sits
A browser extension or script that sends connection requestsProhibited software. It automates activity on the site.
A bot that sends messages or follow-ups in your inboxProhibited software.
A tool that visits profiles in bulk or collects profile dataScraping, which LinkedIn does not permit.
A tool that logs in with your credentials and clicks around for youProhibited, and it also means handing your login to a third party.
Drafting a post in a document or an AI tool, then reviewing it yourselfOutside LinkedIn. No rule against it.
Scheduling a post with LinkedIn's own schedulerSupported by LinkedIn.
Scheduling through a tool LinkedIn lists as a marketing partnerA supported route. Check the tool's access and what it is permitted to do.

What happens when an account is restricted

LinkedIn's help pages say that members who use prohibited tools risk having their accounts restricted or closed, and that the tools themselves may stop working without notice. For automation-related restrictions, LinkedIn describes lifting the restriction after the offending software or extension is disabled. It also warns that repeated restrictions can lead to more serious action, so a restored account is not permission to start again.

For a founder, the real cost is not the notice. It is a week or more without access to the place where prospects, hires and partners find you, during a period you did not pick. If your LinkedIn profile is your main business asset, a shortcut that puts it at risk is poor value.

Why connection and message automation is the riskiest part

Most restrictions that people write about involve outreach: bulk connection requests, templated messages and follow-up sequences run by a tool. These do the two things LinkedIn most wants to prevent, which are acting at a volume no person would and sending messages that recipients did not ask for. LinkedIn also limits how many invitations a member can send in a week, and it does not publish the exact figure, so any number quoted by a tool vendor is an estimate. Treat them with caution.

The better approach to outreach is slower and more human. Write each connection note yourself, mention something specific about the person, and keep a simple list of who you contacted and when. Software can help you prepare, for example by drafting a note for you to edit or reminding you who to follow up with, but a person should press send.

What you can safely automate

Most of the effort in a LinkedIn presence is not clicking. It is deciding what to say, writing it, getting it approved and keeping a steady rhythm. All of that sits outside the platform, and all of it can be automated or assisted.

  • Topic planning. Turn your notes, client questions and past calls into a queue of post ideas.
  • First drafts. Draft posts from your own expertise, in your voice, with your examples. The draft is a starting point for your edit.
  • A review queue. Keep drafts in one place with a status: idea, drafted, approved, scheduled, posted.
  • Scheduling through supported routes. Use LinkedIn's own scheduler or a tool LinkedIn lists as a scheduling partner.
  • Reporting. Pull the numbers LinkedIn shows you into a weekly summary of what worked.

How to judge a LinkedIn tool before you use it

  1. Ask where it runs. If it lives in your browser, clicks buttons on LinkedIn or needs your LinkedIn password, treat it as prohibited software.
  2. Ask how it publishes. A tool that uses an approved route and says so plainly is safer than one that avoids the question.
  3. Be wary of promises about "safe limits" or "undetectable" behaviour. If a tool is built to avoid detection, it is built around breaking the rules.
  4. Keep a person in the loop. Approve every post, and write your own connection notes and messages.
  5. Keep your credentials to yourself. Never share a LinkedIn login with a tool or a freelancer you cannot verify.

A safe weekly routine for a founder

A routine that stays inside the rules takes about an hour a week and does not depend on any tool acting on LinkedIn for you.

  1. Collect (10 minutes). Note down the questions customers asked this week, a decision you made and a mistake you learnt from. These are your raw material.
  2. Draft (15 minutes). Turn two or three of those notes into drafts, with or without AI help. Keep one specific example in each.
  3. Review (15 minutes). Read each draft aloud. Remove anything you would not say to a customer, check every fact and number, and add the detail only you know.
  4. Schedule (5 minutes). Queue the approved posts through LinkedIn's own scheduler or a supported scheduling partner.
  5. Engage (15 minutes). Reply to comments and write a few thoughtful comments on other people's posts yourself. This is the part that cannot be handed to software and is the part readers notice.

Questions people ask

Is LinkedIn automation allowed?

LinkedIn does not permit third-party software, including bots, crawlers and browser extensions, that scrapes data or automates activity on the site, and accounts that use it risk being restricted. Drafting, planning and reviewing content outside LinkedIn is not automation of the platform, and you post it yourself or through a supported route.

Can you get banned for using LinkedIn automation tools?

Yes. LinkedIn's help pages say members who use prohibited tools risk having their accounts restricted or shut down, and the tools may stop working without notice. LinkedIn says restricted accounts are generally restored once the offending software is disabled, but repeated restrictions can lead to permanent action.

Can AI write my LinkedIn posts?

Nothing in LinkedIn's published rules stops you using AI to help draft a post. The risk is quality and honesty rather than the rules: keep your own experience, numbers and opinions in it, and have a person read and approve every post before it goes out.

Is it safe to schedule LinkedIn posts?

Scheduling through LinkedIn's own scheduler or through tools LinkedIn lists as scheduling partners is the supported route. A tool that logs in as you and clicks buttons in your browser is not.

Does LinkedIn allow automated connection requests and messages?

No. Sending connection requests or messages with bots, scripts or browser extensions falls under the prohibited software that LinkedIn describes. Connection requests and direct messages are better written by a person, with AI at most helping you prepare notes about who to contact.
